# Ethical Behavioral Design

Behavioral design can help people complete tasks, learn, discover information, and make decisions. It can also be misused to manipulate.

## Ethical principles

### Transparency
Users should understand important consequences before committing.

### Agency
Users should be able to make meaningful choices without coercion.

### Reversibility
Important decisions should be changeable or cancellable when reasonably possible.

### Proportionality
Persuasive techniques should be proportionate to legitimate user goals.

### Accessibility
Behavioral mechanisms must not exploit disability, confusion, age-related limitations, or other vulnerabilities.

### Privacy
Do not use psychological insights to pressure users into unnecessary data disclosure.

## Avoid dark patterns

Do not recommend:
- hidden fees
- bait-and-switch
- disguised ads
- forced continuity
- obstruction of cancellation
- confirmshaming
- deceptive urgency
- fake scarcity
- confusing opt-outs
- preselected high-cost options that are difficult to change
- misleading countdowns
- intentionally confusing wording

## Ethical persuasion

A persuasive interface can still be ethical when it:
- explains benefits accurately
- makes alternatives visible
- presents meaningful information
- lets users decline
- avoids deceptive pressure
- respects the user's stated goals

## Review test

Before shipping a behaviorally persuasive interaction, ask:

1. Would a reasonable user understand what is happening?
2. Is the important consequence visible before commitment?
3. Can the user say no without unreasonable friction?
4. Can the user change their decision?
5. Would the design still feel acceptable if shown publicly?
6. Does the mechanism serve the user's goal as well as the organization's goal?

If the design depends on users misunderstanding it, the mechanism should be reconsidered.
