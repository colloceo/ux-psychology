# UX Psychology Principles

## 1. Mental Models

A mental model is the user's internal understanding of how a system works.

Design implication:
- Match familiar conventions where possible.
- Use domain language users understand.
- Explain unfamiliar behavior.
- Keep interactions predictable.

Review question:
> What does the user expect will happen next?

## 2. Cognitive Load

Cognitive load is the mental effort required to process information and complete a task.

Three useful categories:
- Intrinsic load: complexity inherent to the task.
- Extraneous load: effort caused by poor presentation or unnecessary complexity.
- Germane load: effort invested in forming useful understanding.

Reduce extraneous load through hierarchy, chunking, progressive disclosure, clear defaults, and concise instructions.

## 3. Recognition vs Recall

Recognition gives users visible options or contextual information. Recall requires users to retrieve information from memory.

Prefer:
- visible labels
- autocomplete
- recent items
- examples
- contextual help
- sensible defaults

## 4. Attention

Attention is limited and selective.

Use:
- visual hierarchy
- whitespace
- grouping
- meaningful contrast
- clear primary actions

Do not make every element visually urgent.

## 5. Memory

Working memory is limited. Externalize information where possible.

Useful techniques:
- show progress
- preserve entered information
- summarize before confirmation
- use consistent placement and terminology

## 6. Von Restorff Effect

A distinctive item is more likely to be noticed or remembered among similar items.

Use sparingly for:
- primary actions
- genuinely important warnings
- key differentiators

Do not make multiple competing elements equally distinctive.

## 7. Serial Position Effect

People tend to remember items near the beginning and end of a sequence better than items in the middle.

Applications:
- important navigation items can occupy prominent positions
- critical information can be reinforced at the end of a flow
- avoid burying essential information in long lists

## 8. Hick's Law

Decision time tends to increase as the number and complexity of choices increase.

Use:
- meaningful grouping
- progressive disclosure
- recommended paths when appropriate
- filters and search
- fewer simultaneous choices

Do not remove legitimate choices merely to force a conversion.

## 9. Fitts's Law

The time required to reach a target is affected by its size and distance.

Use appropriately sized controls, adequate spacing, and touch-friendly targets.

## 10. Feedback Loops

Users need to understand whether an action was received, processed, completed, or failed.

Good feedback:
- immediate
- specific
- proportional
- actionable

## 11. Affordances and Signifiers

An affordance describes what an object can support; a signifier communicates how it can be used.

Examples:
- button styling signals clickability
- handles signal dragging
- labels signal input requirements

## 12. Gestalt Principles

Common grouping principles include:
- proximity
- similarity
- continuity
- closure
- figure-ground
- common region

Use grouping to communicate structure without unnecessary visual clutter.

## 13. Zeigarnik Effect

People may retain interrupted or incomplete tasks more strongly than completed ones.

Ethical applications:
- save unfinished work
- show meaningful progress
- provide clear continuation paths

Avoid manufacturing artificial incompleteness merely to pressure users.

## 14. Peak-End Rule

People's retrospective evaluation of an experience is influenced strongly by salient moments and the ending.

Improve:
- key moments of interaction
- completion and confirmation
- error recovery
- final summaries

Do not use a polished ending to conceal a fundamentally poor experience.
